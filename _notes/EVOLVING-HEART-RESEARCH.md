# The evolving heart — research pass

**Question (author, 2026-08-22, research-first):** "how do we handle the 'heart' of the city
evolving since the bowl will become something else, then something else, and eventually an
arcology … a smooth way … without too much striking, building, moving to make room … maybe plots
can be 'relocated' for a 'knock down, move over' cost that's just labour and time? … it should
probably be supported by research"

**Under test:** (1) the author's RELOCATION verb; (2) the orchestrator's RESERVED RING + YIELDING
PLOT sketch (`BUILDING-CATALOGUE-BRIEF.md`, final section).

**Method:** praise-first comparables — find what players LOVE about central-structure growth and
settlement reflow, then mine the negative space. Every co-opt runs the four-part filter: Qud
fiction / our pillars (protection law, Addendum 8 time doctrine, consent-before-cost) /
not-already-built / implementable in the plot+layout+scaffold machinery that ships.

**Author steer applied mid-pass (2026-08-22, now pinned in the brief):** *"lets not overfit to anno
too hard, this is caves of qud remember"*; **one megastructure per city by default**, the arcology
as singular end-state, with specialised end-states possible only on justification that survives the
pillars — *never a build-more-wonders lane*; and the scale ruling (a zone is one of a parasang's
nine 80×25 sections; the arcology spans *a parasang and then some*). The recommendation in §3 was
re-cut against this: see **§3.0** (the register — accretion, not upgrade), **§3e(i)** (the radius
left in the quarry), **§3e-bis** (the anti-overfit audit), and rejections **15–17**.

Status: RESEARCH ONLY. Nothing here is ruled. Sections 2 and 3 are recommendations to the author.

## Findings in brief

1. **The author's relocation instinct is right, and better-evidenced than expected.** Farthest
   Frontier ships the exact rule — *"Relocating a building is free, apart from some labor"* — and its
   community treats the question as settled rather than contentious.
2. **But the cost model is not the point.** The survey's headline finding: the axis players actually
   care about is **whether the move preserves the thing's IDENTITY** — workers, contents, upgrades,
   orders, name. *"No game surveyed cleanly preserves"* that list. Dwarf Fortress: *"There is no such
   thing as moving a workshop."* **We can preserve it, cheaply, because residents already bind by
   plot id.** That is the differentiator, not the price tag.
3. **Reservation is unserved by every game in the survey**, and asked for by name in two of them —
   Frostpunk players want Factorio ghosts to plan without spending; Dwarf Fortress's own official
   guide tells you to *"leave room to enlarge it in at least one direction in the future"* while the
   game gives you no way to record that.
4. **The ring's geometry is the part to refuse.** Frostpunk's forced radial layout is the exact
   churn the author is trying to avoid (*"the game wants them to demolish the city to reorganize
   stuff"*), and a hard ring contradicts the shipped ruling that a staked plan beats the grammar.
   Reserve **the heart's own plot**, not a ring of the map.
5. **The biggest simplification available:** if the heart's full ground is surveyed at the founding
   rite and staked a rung at a time, **rungs 1–4 need no relocation at all** — the heart grows into
   ground it already holds, and the unbuilt part is the plaza meanwhile. Relocation becomes a rare
   late verb, not the growth mechanism.
6. **And the register the author's steer forces is the best answer of all: the rungs ACCRETE rather
   than replace.** A town hall that levels up is Anno's shape; Qud's is sediment — the Yd Freehold
   built *"over the course of hundreds of years"*, Ezra as an *"archaeological and cultural
   outgrowth"* of the Tomb. Keep the basin, build the kerb around it, raise the hall over the kerb,
   the colonnade around the hall, the arcology over all of it with the court as its atrium. **Almost
   nothing is ever struck, and the ground itself becomes readable history.** §3.0.

**Consequence for the design:** the heart ladder should read as *the settlement becoming a place
like the ones Qud already has*, and its top rung should be named in Qud's own morphology, not
called "arcology tier 4" in the UI.

| **`GrowthStage`**: Camp / Steading / Village / Town / City, plot sizes S/M/L/XL gated by stage. | `Core/KingdomRules.cs:3-10`; `Growth/KingdomPlotRules*.cs` | Five stages, four plot sizes — the heart ladder should ride these rungs rather than invent a sixth axis. |

**One flag found while reading:** `PlotIdProperty` is minted as `Key + "@" + Rect.X1 + "." +
Rect.Y1 + "." + TimeTicks` (`Growth/KingdomPlot2*.cs`, `PlotIdProperty`). It is used purely as an opaque grouping key,
so a relocation that keeps the id is correct — but the id would then *describe* coordinates it no
longer occupies. Cosmetic today; worth minting a coordinate-free id before a move verb ships, so
nothing later is tempted to parse it.

### 1b. Frostpunk 1 & 2, and Against the Storm — the two games that ARE this question

*(Sourcing caveat from the research pass: Reddit and the ATS/Frostpunk official wikis were
unreachable; everything below is Steam forums, Metacritic user reviews, dev blogs, and press.
Numbers marked player-derived where they are.)*

#### Frostpunk 1 — the Generator

**Mechanic.** A circular crater with the coal Generator at dead centre and a **polar build grid**:
buildings sit on concentric rings. Two independent dials — **heat level** (4 steps, hotter) and
**range** (4 steps, wider, each ≈ one more buildable ring, unlocked by three techs) — plus
**Overdrive** (instant +1 heat, no coal, accumulates stress until the Generator breaks). **Steam
Hubs** are satellite emitters whose zones **do not stack** with the Generator's. Lead designer Kuba
Stokalski: *"Heat means survival so the generator is the life-saving heart of our society… Radial
shapes surrounding it have been established to be a pretty important factor of Frostpunk design
process."* ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2019/09/20/frostpunk-console-edition-radial-driven-design-of-gameplay-and-controls/))
**There is no relocation at all.** "Can you move buildings rather than demolishing and rebuilding?"
→ *"You cant."* Demolish refunds partially.
([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696045708653207676/),
[thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/3374907062173860754/))

**PRAISED — and this is the single most useful quote in the whole pass:**

> **"Whenever I upgrade the range, I can be sure that whatever house sits halfway within the new
> ring, is neatly affected. I never have to rebuild."** — Skirlasvoud
> ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696046342866365121/))

That is praise for **predictable reflow**. The loved version of "the heart grows" is one the player
could *pre-position for*. Also praised: the constraint is generative — the designer analysis notes
that because range must be upgraded *"you cannot plan your whole city ahead of time… you need to
build with the constraints you have and adapt"*, and the forced imperfection *"gives the player a
reason to remove and replace non-optimized buildings"*
([Bailly](https://david-bailly.com/portfolio/imperfect-placement-in-circular-city-building-frostpunk/)).
And players defend the untidiness as character: *"It looks much more organic and better imo if you
have gaps in between and things dont line up straight"* — Etzak.

**CURSED.**
- The polar grid does not tile. *"The hardest part of this game to me so far is dealing with the
  layout of my city around the generator and my OCD. Unused space sets off my inner rage."* —
  DmitriTheDemon. The sharpest form: **"What's the point of a grid then? They could just have it
  blank and you place your buildings anywhere."** — Rikashey
  ([1](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696045708638590515),
  [2](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696046342852958860/))
- **Growing the heart is dominated by decentralising it.** *"I've never researched any of the range
  upgrades. Just doesn't seem worth it."* — Bukkfrig. *"Range extension is as useless as usual."* —
  Shining Hector. Finishing the Generator in Endless was called **"anticlimactic"** because it
  *"provides zero immediate benefit."*
- No relocation → mass demolition. On Winterhome: *"i am forced to tear down everything sooner or
  later and build the city from scratch, because its unspeakably iritating to manage this
  abomination of a city."*
  ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1735465524700549252?ctp=3))

**NEGATIVE SPACE — three of the five asks are our two proposals verbatim:**
1. Move a building for a cost. Asked repeatedly; answer is always "you can't."
2. **Factorio-style ghost/blueprint planning** — player *Graycor* asked to mark future medical,
   food, housing and road zones at **zero resource cost** and commit only what is needed now,
   motivated explicitly by *"the frustration of having to demolish large numbers of buildings later
   in the game when consolidating."* The community workaround today is to **place a building and
   pause its construction indefinitely as a reservation.**
3. **A preview of the next ring's footprint before you commit to the current one.** That is the
   surveyed ring, requested by name.
4. Lock a building to its minimum footprint (auto-expand eats reserved gaps).

#### Frostpunk 2 — the control experiment, and the closest shipped precedent

**Mechanic.** Hexes; you zone 6-tile **Districts** rather than place buildings; expansions of 3
tiles unlock building slots; heat becomes a **per-district allocation** with adjacency bonuses.
**Relocation exists in a form startlingly close to our proposal:** a district's area can be
**Adjusted for 50 Heatstamps + 50 Prefabs, moving any number of tiles to a new location — except
the first tile placed when the district was founded, which can never be moved.** Demolition takes
three weeks, refunds 75%, and cannot be cancelled.

**CURSED — loss of authorship, and it is the loudest signal in the corpus:**

> *"you no longer build a city — you choose tiles on which the game creates a type of district.
> **Players don't feel like they built the city because they didn't.**"*
>
> *"You build in tiles… **The city builds itself… its weird**."* — godder, Metacritic 4
>
> a player **"stopped playing because they removed a core function of the game which is balancing
> the heat mechanics with your city layout."**
>
> *"feels impersonal and anonymous"* — Trishi, Metacritic 6
> ([Metacritic user reviews](https://www.metacritic.com/game/frostpunk-2/user-reviews/))

**The lesson, stated plainly: when the central structure's output stops being a SPATIAL constraint
and becomes a NUMERIC one, the settlement stops being a shape the player authored.** FP2's
"un-movable founding tile" is also cursed as arbitrary — which is precisely the objection our
fiction answers, because our founding tile is *where water was shared in front of witnesses*.

#### Against the Storm — the Hearth

**Mechanic.** The **Ancient Hearth is pre-placed and can never be moved.** Each hearth projects a
radius (~9 tiles) and **radii may never overlap**, so the settlement grows as discrete islands, not
a blob. Houses must be inside a radius or villagers will not live in them; if the fire goes out the
homes in radius **become deserted**. A Firekeeper burns fuel; the Ancient Hearth's firekeeper
species grants a settlement-wide bonus. **Hub levels 1–3** (Encampment / Neighbourhood / District)
are earned by **housed villagers plus graded decorations plus a service building, all entirely
inside the radius**, and their bonuses stack globally across hearths. Small Hearths cost
**Wildfire Essence**, a non-producible resource, and each burns as much fuel as the first.
**Relocation (press M):** camps/fishing huts/decorations **free**; houses and farms **for a
resource cost**; industrial and city buildings **not at all**; repaired ruins only under the rare
"Levitating Monument" map modifier; the Ancient Hearth **never**. Dismantling refunds materials in
full.

**PRAISED.**
- Free relocation is called **"a joyous revelation"** — you can adjust the town without penalty
  ([GameWatcher](https://www.gamewatcher.com/reviews/against-the-storm-review/13353)).
- **The heart's tier is a function of what you built around it**, and it reads as elegant:
  *"I could upgrade my hearth by placing housing and decorations around it."* — Adrian Hon
  ([substack](https://adrianhon.substack.com/p/against-the-storm)). Dismantle-and-refund is praised
  too: *"moving smaller campsites as often as you like when you encounter better resources."*
- Secondary hearths read as deliberate colonisation: *"Ruins typically become the start of a new
  Small Hearth and habitation hub."* — Hariman
  ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1336490/discussions/0/4628107223585813832/))
- The devs' rationale for anchoring needs to the hearth is a direct argument for a spatial anchor:
  villagers meeting needs in individual houses *"goes against one of the fundamental principles of
  Against the Storm — being concerned with the settlement as a whole and not with individual
  villagers,"* and produced *"more micromanagement, less flavor, and a constant fear of nonoptimal
  house placement."* ([Eremite](https://eremitegames.com/hubs-system/))
- **Players defend the LIMITS on relocation as load-bearing:** *"If you can constantly move building
  at any time, it sort of removes quite a bit of structure… It would remove some of the actual
  decision tree in your city layout."*
  ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1336490/discussions/3/3819658968015210794/));
  *"This is a trade-off, and an opportunity for you to make a decision."* — HouseOfTheRat.

**CURSED.**
- **The non-overlap rule collides with terrain** — the #1 hearth complaint. *"the fact that you cant
  overlap the radius and it has to be the very edge of the nearest dangerous glade is
  frustrating"*; the player asks for *"a bomb to blow up an area for construction"* and names the
  double-bind: penalised both for taking time **and** for clearing space
  ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1336490/discussions/0/4040355479657537845)).
- **Nobody knows what the radius gates.** *"Does my entire building have to be in that radius? Just
  one tile? None of the above?"* — Singular They. *"Mechanics are just a little bit obtuse/not how
  you'd expect them to work."*
- **The top rung is not worth its ground.** *"The last upgrade is very meh — usually you are better
  off having multiple level 1 or 2 hubs."*
- **A fixed structure in the wrong place hard-blocks the hub tier** — a player needed a service
  building for the level-3 upgrade, but the only one was a repaired ruin far out in a suburb. Ask:
  *"after I repair a big building, I get the option to move it into my settlement!"*
- **The demolish-refund workaround is materially free and still resented:**
  > **"we have to re-add the staff, re setup any restrictions and limits, and in general do a ton
  > more clicks."** … **"I'd be happy to pay a couple planks to be able to move a warehouse, and
  > save myself the clicks of rebuilding."** — Nanako, *"Make everything movable at some cost"*
  > ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1336490/discussions/3/3728450017272563283/))
- And: **"Even though you can move buildings, you can't move workers. They remain where they were."**
  — Sverr.

**NEGATIVE SPACE:** *"Make everything movable at some cost"* is the most-repeated ask, and its
framing matters — players do **not** want free movement, they want a **priced** move that **skips
the re-configuration tax**. Also: move a repaired ruin once for a cost; move a building still under
construction; a valid-placement preview *before* the irreversible act; a UI that states what the
radius actually gates. And one absence that is itself a finding: **nobody ever proposes moving the
Ancient Hearth.** The immovable heart is so assumed it is not even wished against.

#### The cross-cutting finding — and why it lands squarely on our machinery

**The relocation tax players actually resent is CONFIGURATION, not materials.** Two communities
converge on it independently: Frostpunk has no move at all, and Against the Storm has a *free* move
that players still call "a pain" — because you re-staff, re-set limits, and the workers do not
follow. The design both communities are asking for is: **relocation costs resources, preserves
worker assignments and settings, and is refused for a small named set of buildings.**

We are unusually well placed to ship exactly that, and by accident of good data modelling:
`HomePlotIdProperty` binds residents to a **plot id**, not to a cell or an object
(`Growth/KingdomLodging.cs:74,393`). A move that keeps the id moves the household **with** the
house. Against the Storm's loudest relocation complaint — *"you can't move workers, they remain
where they were"* — cannot happen here. **That, not the cost model, is the product.**

### 1c. Relocation across ~20 builders, and Dwarf Fortress pre-planning

*(Sourcing caveat: Reddit, Nexus and Timberborn's FeatureUpvote were unreachable for this pass;
evidence is Steam forums and reviews, official wikis, Bay12/Factorio forums, dev statements, press.)*

#### The headline finding — and it reframes the author's question

The survey's own conclusion, which is worth quoting in full because it is the answer to the
question the author actually asked:

> **The "cheap relocate trivializes planning" debate is not the real axis. The axis is: does the
> move preserve the IDENTITY of the thing — workers, contents, upgrades, work orders, name — and
> does it cost LABOUR-TIME rather than materials?** Games that force demolish+rebuild are cursed
> *not* because of resource loss (most refund generously) but because **the rebuilt thing is a
> different thing.**

And the gap it leaves open:

> **"No game surveyed cleanly preserves name, assigned workers, stored contents, upgrades, and job
> queues across a relocation. This is the biggest open gap and the highest-leverage thing a mod can
> nail."**

Worked examples of the failure, across four games:
- **Dwarf Fortress:** *"To you as the player it is 'moving' the workshop, but as far as the game is
  concerned you removed a workshop and built another workshop somewhere else… **There is no such
  thing as moving a workshop.**"* — Fel
  ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/975370/discussions/0/4330853251140773304)). Workshop-bound
  work orders die with the building.
- **Airborne Empire:** disassembly gives a full resource refund, but **"anything you spent on
  upgrades is lost."**
- **Manor Lords:** demolish the Manor to move it → the displaced families go **inactive** and nobody
  moves back in
  ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1363080/discussions/0/4358998287111340656/)).
- **Timberborn:** move the district centre → membership recomputes and buildings defect to the
  wrong district.
- **Ostriv**, the hardest-line no-move design in the survey, nevertheless shipped a separate
  **"Relocate to another building"** verb *just for occupants* — which is what finally allowed its
  previously-undemolishable Town Hall to be removed. **Even the strictest game caved specifically
  on the anchor structure.**

#### Farthest Frontier — the author's proposal, already shipped and well received

> Official rule: **"Relocating a building is free, apart from some labor."** (Town Hall costs gold.)
> Moving hurts nearby house desirability.
> — [farthestfrontier.com guide](https://www.farthestfrontier.com/guide/gameplay/buildings/)

The threads read as **resolved, not aggrieved** — *"if you have placement issues, you should just
move it."* The residual complaint is not about the tool at all; it is about **information at
placement time**: *"its a shame we cant reposition all of our buildings to take advantage of new
information not on the initial placement area."* No player in the reachable threads complained that
it trivializes planning.

#### PRAISED

- **Factorio's cut & paste:** *"In my opinion, this was the best update the game ever received."* —
  Nailfoot ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/427520/discussions/0/3103516354864827939/)).
  And the honest framing from the same thread: *"No, you cannot move buildings — you can only order
  the old buildings to be deconstructed and new buildings to be constructed somewhere else."*
  **The move is fictional; the labour is real. Players are delighted anyway, because the interaction
  is one gesture.**
- **Construction-as-labour is loved when it is legible.** Workers & Resources top reviews: *"You
  design it, fund it, schedule trucks to haul gravel, hire men to spread it…"*, *"It's HOW YOU BUILD
  A ROAD!!!"*, **"Every piece of progress feels earned, not given."** The community fights about
  labour *allocation opacity*; **nobody argues construction should be instant.**
- **Move It's popularity is about precision and coverage, not price.** Vanilla C:S already relocates
  service buildings for ~25%. The mod's subscribers are buying sub-tile precision, rotation, and
  coverage of things vanilla refuses to move — *"it would be like release from a prison of strange
  physics."*
- **Timberborn's anchor move:** *"the very first thing I did was relocate the district centre… **made
  a massive change in the game.**"* — jaquig
  ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1062090/discussions/0/2953788788213141512/))

#### CURSED — and this is where the anti-camp's real concerns live

The pro/anti argument is genuinely live. The strongest anti quotes:

> *"there has to be consequences to placing buildings otherwise it becomes a bit meaningless."*
> — Sam Look, Kingdoms and Castles
> ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/569480/discussions/0/1458455461483916714))
>
> *"intelligent placement of buildings… is… foundational"*; a move tool would *"defeat the
> purpose"*; losing resources is *"very intentional."* — Summersong, Timberborn
> ([thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1062090/discussions/0/4845400193816621590/))
>
> *"If you can just move structures like magic after they're built with little to no effort, it
> would not only break the immersion, but remove challenge, core gameplay elements, and replay
> aspects."* — Axan436, ASKA
>
> *"That mod is a tough one because its essentially a cheat depending on how one chooses to use
> it."* — Cities: Skylines 2 forum

**But read carefully, the anti-camp almost never defends "you must eat the material cost." It
defends three separable things:**

| What they are protecting | The evidenced fix |
|---|---|
| **Timing exploits** — repositioning during a flood (Timberborn) or during combat (Airborne Empire's dev rationale) | **The move takes in-world time and is interruptible.** Kills the objection outright. |
| **Physics / collision violation** — the C:S "super cheaty" concession is about *anarchy*, not about undoing mistakes | **The move obeys every placement rule the original build obeyed.** |
| **Diegetic plausibility** — *"Moving an entire building doesn't make sense"*; *"would definitely have landed you at the stake"* (Bellwright) | **Make the fiction do the work.** A crew dismantling and re-raising over days is plausible; teleporting is not. Factorio's answer, and nobody objects. |

**And what actually destroys weight is not cost — it is instantaneity + universality + zero
side-effects.** Four levers, any one sufficient: **time** (Farthest Frontier), **difficulty-scaled
price** (Anno), **scope** (Manor Lords — relocation is allowed exactly where *"the information was
not available at placement time"*), **place-gating** (Airborne Empire's skydock).

#### Dwarf Fortress — how players pre-plan, and what it costs them

DF's three cost tiers explain its whole planning culture: **designations/zones/stockpiles are free
to redraw; dug space is free to extend and impossible to un-dig; buildings return materials but
destroy identity.**

**PRAISED — plan the finished fort from day one.**
> *"You should plan for your finished, 200-dwarf fortress right from the get-go, as it's very easy
> to dig out new area but very hard to go back and redo something the way it should have been from
> the start."*

And reservation is written into the **official Quickstart guide**
([link](https://www.dwarffortresswiki.org/Quickstart_guide)):
> *"leave room to enlarge it in at least one direction in the future"* … *"leave empty space on at
> least one side of your dining hall so that it can be expanded later if necessary."*

**The single most transferable idea in the whole research pass** — from the workshop-design wiki
([link](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Workshop_design)):
> *"this plan is only meant to expand to the right and bottom-right. So you can safely plug its top
> and left sides flush with the rest of your fortress without worrying about future expansion."*

**A published growth direction lets a player pack tightly without fear.** And the survey's own
negative-space note: *"Nobody has made growth-direction a first-class, machine-readable property of
a building."*

**CURSED.** Bulk pre-digging makes miners thrash across z-levels; the community's own fix is
**"do the entire plan as a blueprint first, and convert one level at a time to 'real' orders"** —
staged commitment, invented by players because no game offers it. And the community is genuinely
split: veterans say plan for 200 dwarves, the official Design-strategies page says *"Don't plan
**too** far ahead… focus on modular and compact designs."* The synthesis the evidence supports:
**plan the envelope early, commit the contents late.**

#### NEGATIVE SPACE — the ranked unmet wants

1. **"Move it and keep it the same thing."** The most-repeated, least-served want in the corpus.
   *No surveyed game preserves name + workers + contents + upgrades + queues across a move.*
2. **"Move the plan, not the building."** Moving an unbuilt blueprint costs nothing and risks
   nothing. Workers & Resources players asked for it; the devs marked it **"Work in progress"**; the
   current workaround is *"bulldoze and repeat several times."*
3. **"Reserve this space; don't build here yet."** **DF has no reservation primitive at all** — you
   can only dig it or remember it — while its own official guide begs you to leave room to enlarge.
   The want is real and the tooling is absent everywhere.
4. **"Declare which way this grows."** Only DF's wiki does it, informally, in prose.
5. **"Release the plan a slice at a time."** Player-invented in DF; offered by nobody.
6. **"Relocation as an unlock, not a default."** Airborne Empire's skydock and Terra Nil's
   tool-gated recycling tech are the only two examples of teardown/move *capability* being earned.
   It *"neatly resolves the 'trivializes early planning' objection: early game is committal, late
   game is fluid."*
7. **"Give me the information before I place, not after."** The residual complaint in every game
   that *does* have relocation. **Relocation is often a workaround for an information problem.**
8. **Multi-select move.** Single-building moves don't solve "my whole district is in the wrong
   place."

#### Two warnings we should heed by name

- **Timberborn's anchor lesson:** when the anchor moves, the expensive part is not the building —
  it is **re-deriving every membership and ownership relation that was defined relative to the
  anchor.** Our heart is a *derived centroid* feeding siting, roads, gate and reach; if it moved by
  player action, all of that would have to re-derive. **This is a hard argument for the rite ground
  never moving.**
- **Terra Nil's tell:** its late-game general-purpose recycling drone *"makes recycling so easy it
  almost feels like cheating"* — players say it themselves the moment teardown becomes fully
  general. Scope matters more than price.

## 2. Verdict on the two proposals

### 2a. The author's RELOCATION verb — **SUPPORT, with four refinements**

**"Maybe plots can be 'relocated' for a 'knock down, move over' cost that's just labour and time."**

*Supported by evidence, strongly.*

1. **The absence of this verb is the single most-demanded thing in the genre.** Cities: Skylines'
   *Move It* has **3M+ Steam Workshop subscribers** and is described as granting "a level of
   control that feels like it should have been in the base game all along." The stated reason is
   ours almost word for word: *"Players hate plowing over high level buildings to make room for
   roads"*, and it "lets you move your nice level 5 residences that took forever."
   ([Move It guide](https://attractmo.de/game-mods/cities-skylines-move-it-mod-guide),
   [Nexus](https://www.nexusmods.com/citiesskylines/mods/144)) Note the shape of vanilla's partial
   answer: C:S *already* relocates **public buildings and parks for a cost** — it just refuses to
   relocate the things players are emotionally attached to. Our heart ladder is exactly the
   "public building" case, and our houses are exactly the "level 5 residence" case.
2. **Frostpunk is the counter-example the author is trying to avoid, and its players say so.**
   Players report *"the game wants them to demolish the city to reorganize stuff"* and describe
   having to demolish a huge number of buildings to rebuild more efficiently later
   ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1639788130289434897)).
   Frostpunk has the strongest heart in the genre and the worst reflow story; the two are related.
3. **Free-refund precedent exists and is loved.** Oxygen Not Included refunds deconstruction
   fully, and the praise is exactly "freedom to experiment and reposition without resource
   penalties" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/457140/discussions/0/1732087824987143887/)).
   **But** the same community argues against the *unconditional* version: a long-running thread
   asks that **deconstructing damaged buildings should NOT give full resources**
   ([Klei forums](https://forums.kleientertainment.com/forums/topic/104981-deconstructing-damaged-buildings-shouldnt-give-full-resources/)).
   Players themselves want the refund to respect the object's condition.

**Refinement 1 — conserve materials, but CARRY THE WEAR.** "Labour and time only" is right for the
*materials* (they are the same stones), and the Klei thread is the evidence for the other half: a
moved building arrives with the wear it had. You moved a worn house; you have a worn house. This
also keeps the strike/move distinction honest and load-bearing: **striking returns partial
salvage** (brief, "Lifecycle"), **moving conserves in full** — because nothing was destroyed.
Without that split, "move" becomes a materials laundry (strike-by-moving-one-cell).

**Refinement 2 — the move must be a two-phase handover, not a demolish-then-rebuild.** The
sketch's promise ("no roof brink on a planned move; the household walks over with their house") is
already implemented for upgrades: `r_KingdomImprovement` keeps the predecessor **standing and
working** for the entire build and hands contents across only once the successor is on the ground
(`Growth/KingdomUpgrade*.cs`, `PollHandover`). Point that sideways — successor on the *destination*
rect — and displacement is structurally zero, which is what makes the verb feel kind rather than
merely cheap. It also yields a clean, honest refusal when there is no free ground:
**"there is nowhere to set it down"** (7b).

**Refinement 3 — only what the settlement raised may move; player-built work never moves, ever.**
This is not a tuning choice. It is the protection law, and it is the exact line SS2 crosses and
gets punished for. A ring that cannot grow because the founder's own hall stands in it must
**refuse by name and stop**, not "helpfully" relocate it. (Machinery already scoped for this:
`SweepPlotParts` only ever touches objects carrying `KingdomPlotPartProperty` — the protection
law's own exemption; `UpgradeVerdict.NotOurWork` is already the sentence.)

**Time doctrine check (Addendum 8):** a move is `time × labour × infrastructure`. A crewless
settlement moves nothing. Duration scales with tier footprint and material hardness. The move is
scheduled world-time work, announced once, arrestable — inheriting the existing hold-as-is per
work and per zone. It is **not** a timer that matures; it is hands carrying stone, which is what
Addendum 8 sanctions.


**Refinement 4 — the move verb is itself EARNED, not a starting affordance.** This is the answer to
"it should probably be supported by research", and it is the second-best-evidenced item in the pass.
The survey's negative space #6: relocation-as-an-unlock exists in only two games (Airborne Empire's
skydock, Terra Nil's tool-gated recycling drone) and *"neatly resolves the 'trivializes early
planning' objection: early game is committal, late game is fluid."* Ours has a Qud-honest carrier
already: **crew capability** (Addendum 7 — crews derive capability from settler stats) plus the
derived `TechLevel`. A Camp cannot take a house apart and put it back up; a settlement with a
staffed mason's yard and workshop-level craft can. Early placement stays weighty *by fiction*, and
the escape hatch arrives exactly when the city is big enough to need it. **No new gate type: it is
the existing crew + tech + material-chain ladder, applied to a new verb.**

**The evidence that settles the author's cost model.** Farthest Frontier ships the author's exact
rule — *"Relocating a building is free, apart from some labor"* — and its threads read as resolved
rather than aggrieved; no reachable player complained that it trivializes planning. And the survey's
headline finding reframes what the cost is even *for*:

> The axis is not "cheap move vs. expensive move." It is **"does the move preserve the IDENTITY of
> the thing — workers, contents, upgrades, work orders, name — and does it cost LABOUR-TIME rather
> than materials?"** Games that force demolish+rebuild are cursed *not* because of resource loss —
> most refund generously — but because **the rebuilt thing is a different thing.** And: *"No game
> surveyed cleanly preserves name, assigned workers, stored contents, upgrades, and job queues
> across a relocation. This is the biggest open gap and the highest-leverage thing a mod can nail."*

**We can nail it, and largely by accident of existing data shape:** residents bind by
`HomePlotIdProperty` (a plot id, not a cell or object), and `GrowInPlace` already copies plot id,
rect, footprint and roof to a successor. Dwarf Fortress's *"there is no such thing as moving a
workshop"*, Airborne Empire's *"anything you spent on upgrades is lost"*, Manor Lords' families
going inactive, Against the Storm's *"you can't move workers, they remain where they were"* — none
of those can happen here. **Identity preservation is the product; the cost model is just the price
tag.**

**The three things the anti-relocation camp is actually protecting, and our answer to each.** The
camp is real and quotable — *"there has to be consequences to placing buildings otherwise it becomes
a bit meaningless"* (Sam Look), *"intelligent placement… is foundational"* (Summersong), *"it would
not only break the immersion, but remove challenge"* (Axan436). But it almost never defends the
material cost. It defends:

| Their concern | Our answer, already in the design |
|---|---|
| **Timing exploit** (repositioning during a flood; during combat) | The move is **scheduled world-time labour, announced and arrestable**. It cannot be a reflex. Addendum 8 gives us this for free. |
| **Physics / collision violation** (the C:S "cheaty" concession is about anarchy, not undo) | The move goes through `KingdomPlots.Stake` — **every placement rule the original build obeyed** (footprint ≤ plot, lanes, zoning, stratum, sky). |
| **Diegetic plausibility** ("moving a building doesn't make sense") | A crew taking a house apart and re-raising it over days is entirely plausible — and it is what actually happens. Factorio's precedent: *the move is fictional, the labour is real*, and nobody objects. |

**And what actually destroys weight is not cost — it is instantaneity + universality + zero
side-effects.** Four levers, any one sufficient: **time** (Farthest Frontier), **difficulty-scaled
price** (Anno 1800), **scope** (Manor Lords: relocation allowed exactly where *the information was
not available at placement time*), **place-gating** (Airborne Empire). **We hold three of the four**
— time, scope, and (via refinement 4) a capability gate. That is belt, braces, and a second belt.

**Not-already-built check:** PASS. Nothing in `Growth/` moves a plot. `GrowInPlace` grows in place;
`KingdomSocket` restakes on the *same* rect. The move verb is a genuinely new capability built
from three existing pieces.

### 2b. The RESERVED RING + YIELDING PLOT sketch — **SUPPORT the reservation, REFUTE the ring's geometry, REFINE the yielding contract**

**Support — reservation is received wisdom and an explicit player request.**

- The city-builder strategy canon states it plainly: *"Reserve central land for services before
  surrounding it with housing. When you leave space for public facilities early, the city can grow
  around them with fewer expensive rebuilds."*
  ([BuildCity guide](https://buildcity.io/city-builder-guide))
- Frostpunk players asked for precisely the free-ghost half of the sketch: a request for
  **"'ghost buildings' or blueprints similar to Factorio, so players could plan their city layout
  without the cost of immediately building everything"**
  ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1639788130289434897)).
- Factorio validates the mechanic: player-placed ghosts **persist forever and cost nothing**
  ([wiki](https://wiki.factorio.com/Ghost)), and the community's most telling mod is
  **Construction Approvals** — ghosts require **player approval before bots are dispatched**,
  "giving improved control over what parts of the factory get built and when"
  ([mod](https://mods.factorio.com/mod/ConstructionPlannerContinued)). That is consent-before-cost
  as a player-demanded feature, in the genre's most automation-friendly game.
- We already have the object for it: `r_KingdomPlanMarker` is free, lookable, does nothing on its
  own, and is realised only on the ordinary settlement pass. The reserved ring is a *surveyed
  plan*, not a new concept.

**Refute — a fixed geometric RING is wrong for this mod, on two counts.**

1. **The heart is not fixed; it drifts by construction.** `TryHeart` averages the rite ground
   (weight 1) with every non-Defence mark, so by Town stage the heart has walked away from the
   rite ground. A ring pinned at founding will be measuring from the wrong centre exactly when it
   matters, and a ring that re-centres each pass would *itself* be the churn machine the author is
   trying to kill.
2. **A hard ring contradicts a shipped ruling.** The brief: *"Expansion direction is entirely the
   founder's — claims decide, grammar follows, a staked plan beats the grammar anywhere."*
   Frostpunk's radial geometry is exactly the thing our pillars refuse; its players describe being
   made to demolish to reorganise. Import the *reservation*, refuse the *geometry*.

**The refinement that resolves both — make the heart's own weight rise with its tier, and reserve
by PLOT, not by ring.**

- **`RiteHeartWeight` becomes tier-scaled.** Today it is the constant `1`
  (`Growth/KingdomPlotRules*.cs`) — one vote among forty buildings. Make it the heart structure's own
  tier weight (e.g. 1 / 4 / 12 / 40 across the ladder). Consequences: at Camp the heart still
  drifts to where people actually built (correct — a basin on bare ground is not a monument); as
  the great work rises it **pulls the settled centre back onto itself**, and the city visibly
  re-centres on the monument. That is one constant in shipped code, it stabilises the reservation
  automatically, and it is Qud's own fiction: *Ezra is an "archaeological and cultural outgrowth"
  of the Tomb of the Eaters* — the village grew around the great work.
- **Reserve the heart's OWN PLOT, generously, at founding — not a ring of the map.** The brief
  already rules the ceiling is a staking-time choice ("stake big for room to grow… more yard
  meanwhile"), and *yard = plot minus current footprint, recomputed per tier*. So the reservation
  the heart needs is a **plot whose yard is the plaza until the building grows into it**.

  Two layers, so the stage gate stays honest (Camp really does only lay S):
  **(i) SURVEYED at the founding rite** — the full ladder's final surface rect, as free plan
  ghosts, costing nothing and doing nothing (`r_KingdomPlanMarker`'s exact semantics);
  **(ii) STAKED at the current rung** — the plot rect the settlement actually holds, enlarged into
  surveyed ground each time the heart climbs (`StampRect` with a bigger rect; footprint and roof
  already recompute per tier under Addendum 1).

  No new reservation primitive, no ring recomputation, and the reserved ground is *useful the whole
  time* — it is the market ground, the rite ground, where the pilgrims stand. This is the single
  biggest simplification available: **for every rung below the arcology, relocation is not needed
  at all, because the heart grows into ground it already surveyed.** Verified against
  `Core/KingdomFounding.cs:65-71`: founding currently stamps rite coordinates and nothing else, so
  surveying at the rite is an addition to a function that already runs, not a new hook.
- The **surveyed ring survives only as a soft term in a function that already exists.**
  `ScoreRect` already receives `HasRite, RiteX, RiteY` (`Growth/KingdomPlotRules*.cs`) and already
  subtracts a heart-distance term. Add one repulsion term for "inside the surveyed future ground":
  the settlement will not *volunteer* to build there while clear ground exists. Not a refusal —
  a preference. The founder's stake still beats the grammar, as ruled.

- **NEW, and the single most transferable idea found: DECLARED GROWTH DIRECTION.** Dwarf Fortress's
  workshop-design wiki does it in prose — *"this plan is only meant to expand to the right and
  bottom-right. So you can safely plug its top and left sides flush with the rest of your fortress
  without worrying about future expansion."* The survey's own note: *"Nobody has made
  growth-direction a first-class, machine-readable property of a building."* We already have the
  carrier — Addendum 1 gives every tier a declared footprint, and footprint ≤ plot is
  validator-checked. Add a per-design **growth vector** (which sides later tiers expand into), show
  it at staking, and the founder can pack tight against the safe sides without fear. It also makes
  the heart's own reservation legible: the great court grows *this* way, so build over there.
  Cheap, unserved by every game surveyed, and it costs one attribute plus a preview.

**Reservation is unserved everywhere, and the strongest game in the genre begs for it.** The survey
found **no reservation primitive in any surveyed builder** — Dwarf Fortress players can only "dig it
or remember it" — while DF's own **official Quickstart guide** instructs: *"leave room to enlarge it
in at least one direction in the future"* and *"leave empty space on at least one side of your
dining hall so that it can be expanded later if necessary."* Frostpunk players independently asked
for Factorio-style ghosts to do the same. Veterans state the doctrine flatly: *"plan for your
finished, 200-dwarf fortress right from the get-go, as it's very easy to dig out new area but very
hard to go back and redo something the way it should have been from the start."* And the honest
counterweight, from DF's own Design-strategies page: *"Don't plan **too** far ahead… focus on
modular and compact designs."* **The synthesis both sides support: plan the envelope early, commit
the contents late** — which is exactly the surveyed-then-staked split above.

**One warning to heed by name — Timberborn's anchor lesson.** When the anchor moves, the expensive
part is not the building; it is **re-deriving every membership and ownership relation defined
relative to the anchor** (their district centres cause buildings to defect to the wrong district).
Our heart is a derived centroid feeding siting, roads, gate and reach. **This is an independent,
evidence-backed argument for the rite ground never moving** — and for the heart's *weight* rising
rather than its *position* being reassigned.

**Refine — the YIELDING contract, made narrower and louder.**

The sketch is right that consent must be taken at placement. Two changes:

- **Yielding is a property of the plot, not of the ground.** A settlement-raised plot the founder
  deliberately stakes inside surveyed ground is marked yielding **at the confirmation** and says so
  **in its own description, forever** ("staked in the ground the great court is surveyed for; it
  will be moved when the court is called"). Consent told once and readable later, not a hidden flag.
- **With accretion (§3.0), the yielding contract shrinks to almost nothing.** Rungs 1–4 grow into
  ground the heart already surveyed and holds; only the arcology rung — one per city, once per city
  — ever calls for ground a yielding plot is standing on. That is the honest scope of this whole
  mechanism: **one call, once, at the end of a city's life, on ground the founder was told about at
  the moment they staked it.** If it fires more often than that, something upstream is wrong.
- **A ring call never acts; it OFFERS.** Addendum 3 already rules this shape for working
  buildings, and the heart ladder should inherit it verbatim: the founder is told what is ready,
  what stands in its ground, and what the move costs in days — and nothing moves until they say so.
  Forceable from the Charter with the cost disclosed. This is the C:S-*Move It* lesson taken
  correctly: give the player the verb, do not give the game the verb.

## 3. Recommended design — **the heart ACCRETES; the city re-centres on it**

### 3.0 The register, before the mechanics (author steer, 2026-08-22)

> *"lets not overfit to anno too hard, this is caves of qud remember… i think the arcology special
> structure would likely be the only megastructure in a city."* — and the standing rule restated:
> **comparables are quarries, not blueprints. Anno/Frostpunk/SS2 inform the FEEL; Qud's own
> register — ruins, arcologies as the Yd Freehold, history heavier than convenience — decides the
> SHAPE.**

Taking that seriously changes one thing at the root, and it happens to be the best answer to the
author's original question.

**The heart's rungs ACCRETE. They do not replace.** A city-builder's town hall levels up: the small
one vanishes and a bigger one appears on its footprint. That is Anno's shape and it is the wrong
one here, because Qud's own great places are *layered*: the Yd Freehold was built by four beings
*"over the course of hundreds of years"*; Ezra is an *"archaeological and cultural outgrowth"* of
the Tomb; the Tomb itself is twelve stories accumulated **around** the Spindle. Qud's register is
sediment, not upgrade.

So: **the basin is never removed. The waterstone kerb is never removed — the moot hall is built
over it and it becomes the hall's floor. The cairns are never moved — the colonnade is built around
them. The arcology does not replace the great court; it is raised over it, and the court becomes its
atrium, with the kerb still underfoot and the basin still in the middle.** Each rung is a *layer*
that the next rung encloses.

Three consequences, all of them wins:

1. **This is the anti-churn answer the author asked for.** If rungs accrete rather than replace,
   almost nothing is struck. The striking the brief currently accepts ("a heart full of early huts
   is cleared by striking, which is the system working") stops being the mechanism at the centre and
   becomes an edge case at the margin.
2. **It is legible without a UI.** The founder can walk the heart and *read* the city's whole
   history off the ground — the tin basin, the kerb, the hall's beams inside the court's stone, the
   cairns of everyone who died here. "Everything is remembered, twice" gets a third register: the
   ground itself.
3. **It is mechanically cheaper than the alternative.** Accretion is footprint growth within a plot
   (Addendum 1, already ruled and already implemented), plus contents that survive because nothing
   swept them. Replacement would need a demolition path at every rung.

**History heavier than convenience** also prices the move verb. Relocation is granted — but it
**leaves a mark**: the chronicle records it, the source ground stays a socket rather than reverting
to wilderness (already how `KingdomSocket` behaves), and the moved building carries the fact in its
own description forever — *"raised on the old market ground; carried here when the great court was
called."* Convenience is given; the history is kept. That is the Qud trade, and it costs one string.

**One megastructure per city (author ruling).** The arcology is the city's end-state, singular the
way a heart is singular. The ladder is therefore **not a wonders menu** and must never read as one:
there is one heart, it has rungs, and the top rung is the end of that one thing rather than the
first entry in a list of colossi. Specialised end-state megastructures (the city that became its
granary; a temple-city) stay possible only as *a different top rung for the same single heart*, with
the burden of proof on the design — never as a second structure raised alongside.


One sentence: **the heart is one plot whose full ground is SURVEYED at the founding rite and STAKED
a rung at a time; each rung is built OVER the last rather than in place of it, so the ground itself
carries the city's history — and the only thing that ever needs relocating is what the founder
deliberately chose to put in that surveyed ground, moved on consent for labour and days, carrying
its people and its history with it, never destroyed.**

### 3a. The ladder — five rungs on the five stages, in the existing catalogue's own vocabulary

The rungs below are built from keys that already exist in `KingdomBuildings.xml` (`fire`, `hall`,
`bazaar`, `cistern`/`cisternvault`, `temple`, `cairn`, `shrine`) plus one new civic XL chain. **The
founder's basin is kept and displayed at every rung** — the relic in the middle, which is Qud's own
shape (the sacred well at the centre of the Six Day Stilt).

*Scale (author's ruling, 2026-08-22):* a claimable zone is one of the nine 80×25 sections of a
parasang. City is 4 zones today (a rules constant), mature stages reach the full parasang, and the
arcology spans **a parasang and then some** — neighbouring parasangs and strata. Rung 5 is therefore
not one more footprint step; it is the point at which the heart stops being a building on a plot and
becomes the ground the city stands in. The named checks that ruling flags — claim adjacency and
frontier logic across parasang borders, and at stratum seams — are the arcology rung's real
engineering cost, not the footprint.

*Catalogue check:* the four existing `Plot="XL"` designs are `court` (top housing tier),
`waterworks`, `condensery`, `homefarm` — **there is no XL civic or faith design at all today**, so
the top of this ladder is genuinely new rather than a re-label. Note the name collision: `court` is
already the marble-manor housing rung, so rung 4 needs a different key (`moot`, `forum`, or a
Qudish coinage) even though "great court" reads well in prose.

| Rung | Stage | What the player SEES | Footprint / roof | Costs | Gates |

| **4. Each rung also asks for **the settlement around it** — roofed households and works within the
heart's reach (§3e(i)). The heart climbs because the city thrived, not because a list was ticked.

**"Supported by research" resolves cleanly and without a research tree.** `TechLevel` is *derived*
— `TechPointsPerDisk = 1`, `TechPointsPerCertification = 2`, thresholds `{0,2,5,9,14}`
(`KingdomZoningRules.cs:211-232`), and the file states outright that "this mod has no research tree
and does not want one — a tree is a second job." So the arcology needing **arclight** means it
needs fourteen points of *disks taught to keepers and machines certified fit for the grid*: a long
history of salvage brought home and made trustworthy. That is research the founder does by
adventuring, which is the mod's whole spine (VISION: "first an effortful, primitive rung; then
settlers' hands; then great technology recovered and made trustworthy"). **No new gate type is
needed.** `MinZones` is already a parsed, tested gate (`RefusedTerritory`; catalogue already pairs
2/Village, 3/Town, 4/City) and extends to the arcology unchanged.

### 3b. What moves, what never moves

| | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Never moves** | The **rite ground point** (`RiteXProperty`/`RiteYProperty`) — the ladder is anchored to where water was shared, permanently. The **basin** — kept and displayed at every rung. **Every earlier rung's fabric** — the kerb, the hall's beams, the cairns — because rungs accrete rather than replace (§3.0). Anything **player-placed or player-built** — protection law, absolute, no exception, refuse by name and stop. |
| **Never needs to move** | Everything at rungs 1–4, because the heart grows into its own staked yard. This is the design's main claim: **relocation is a rare late verb, not the growth mechanism.** |
| **May move, on consent** | Settlement-raised plots the founder deliberately staked inside surveyed heart ground (marked **yielding** at placement, stated in the plot's description forever), when the arcology rung is called and needs their ground. |
| **Moves with the building** | Plot id (so residents follow with zero re-assignment — `HomePlotIdProperty`), tier, dedication, contents, **wear**, staffing, held/hold-as-is flags, name, chronicle history. **This list IS the feature** — the survey found no game that preserves it, and every community complains about the loss. |

### 3c. The move, concretely

- **Trigger:** never automatic. The founder is told, once, 7b-shaped: *"The great court is ready.
  Three houses stand in its ground. They can be moved — nine days' work, no stone spent."*
  Consent required (Addendum 3's offer shape, inherited verbatim).
- **Cost:** crew-ticks × world-days, scaled by footprint and material hardness. **No materials
  spent, none refunded** — the same stones go back up. Wear carries. A crewless settlement moves
  nothing and says so.
- **Execution:** the two-phase handover. The old building **stands and shelters** for the whole
  build; the new one rises on the destination rect; contents and residents transfer at completion;
  the old ground is swept in one act and left as a socket. **Displacement is structurally zero.**
- **Destination:** chosen by the existing layout grammar (`KingdomLayout.ChooseCell` /
  `KingdomPlots.TryFindRect`), pre-picked and *shown in the offer* so the founder can refuse.
- **Arrestable:** scheduled world-time work, announced, and stoppable — inheriting hold-as-is per
  work and per zone that already ships.
- **Refusals, all by name:** no free ground → *"there is nowhere to set it down."* Player-built in
  the ring → *"the settlement will not move what you raised with your own hands."* No crew →
  *"there are no hands free to carry it."*

### 3d. The code deltas this actually costs

1. **`RiteHeartWeight` becomes tier-scaled** (`Growth/KingdomPlotRules*.cs`) — one constant becomes one
   small function. The city re-centres on the monument as it rises.
2. **One repulsion term in `ScoreRect`** (`Growth/KingdomPlotRules*.cs`) — it already receives
   `HasRite/RiteX/RiteY` and already subtracts a heart-distance term. Surveyed ground becomes a
   preference against, never a refusal.
3. **A `Relocate` verb in `KingdomSocket`** — `SweepPlotParts(source)` + `RestakeOnRect(destination)`
   already exist and `RestakeOnRect` already takes an arbitrary rect; the identity carry is
   `GrowInPlace`'s existing copy-forward; the standing-until-handover is `r_KingdomImprovement`'s
   existing `PollHandover`. Plus a cost rule with zero material spend, and a capability gate reusing
   the crew + `TechLevel` checks the commission path already runs.
4. **One new optional attribute — the growth vector** on a design's tier chain (§3e(ii)), read at
   staking and shown in the preview. Purely additive to the catalogue schema; merge-by-key carries
   it to third-party designs for free.
5. **Heart-rung thresholds expressed in existing terms** (§3e(i)) — roofed households and works
   within reach, read off `HeartPull`/reach and the `Carries` vocabulary. No new counters.

Housekeeping found while reading: mint a **coordinate-free plot id** before a move verb ships —
`PlotIdProperty` is currently `Key@X1.Y1.TimeTicks` (`Growth/KingdomPlot2*.cs`, `PlotIdProperty`) and after a move would
describe coordinates it no longer occupies. Opaque today, a trap tomorrow.

Tests would land in `DevTests/KingdomPlotRulesTests.cs` (weight/repulsion), a new
`KingdomRelocateRulesTests.cs` (cost, refusals, identity carry), and `KingdomFootprintTests.cs`
(footprint growth within a staked heart plot).

### 3e. Four additions the comparables pass forced into the design

**(i) The heart's rung is earned by what the CITY has become — not only by stage and materials.**
Against the Storm's most-praised property is one sentence: *"I could upgrade my hearth by placing
housing and decorations around it"* (Adrian Hon). Their hub levels require housed villagers plus
graded decorations plus a service building, all wholly inside the radius. **The insight worth
quarrying is that the anchor and the sprawl become ONE system. The radius is the part to leave in
the quarry** — a "count what is inside the circle" rule is exactly the town-radius shape the author
warned against, and it would re-import Against the Storm's own #1 complaint (a radius rule
colliding with terrain, with nobody sure what it gates).

**The Qud-register version measures the settlement, not a circle.** A rung asks for what the city
demonstrably *is*: households roofed, works standing and staffed, water held, the level its
infrastructure honestly carries. Every one of those is already computed — stage is already earned
by demonstrated feed, the equilibrium model already says what the works carry, `Carries` already
denominates contribution. **No new counter, no new geometry.** Consequence: the heart climbs
because the city thrived, and the founder reads the rung as a verdict on the settlement rather than
a shopping list — which is also the honest reading of a city that grew a monument.

**(ii) Declared growth direction, as a first-class attribute.** From DF's workshop-design idiom, and
unserved by every game in the survey. Each design's tier chain declares which sides later footprints
expand into; staking shows it. The founder can pack tight against the safe sides deliberately.
Rides Addendum 1's footprint-per-tier and the existing footprint ≤ plot validation.

**(iii) The move verb is earned, not given.** Gated on **crew capability + derived TechLevel**
(Addendum 7 + `KingdomZoningRules.TechPoints`), so early placement stays committal by fiction and
the escape hatch arrives when the city is big enough to need it. This is the second half of the
author's "supported by research", and it needs no new gate type.

**(iv) The top rung must PAY at completion.** Two independent warnings: Frostpunk players called
finishing the Generator **"anticlimactic"** because it *"provides zero immediate benefit"*, and
Against the Storm's top hub level is judged *"very meh — usually you are better off having multiple
level 1 or 2 hubs"* — i.e. **the grandest rung is not worth its ground.** Frostpunk's range upgrade
suffers the same fate: *"I've never researched any of the range upgrades. Just doesn't seem worth
it."* The arcology therefore has to deliver something the city cannot get any other way at the
moment its last stage stamps — the citywide/realm-wide functions of the reach doctrine, the
inherited backbone (riser taps, freight shaft), the surface-impossible sets — and it has to be told
in the chronicle as an accomplishment. **A prestige-only colossus is the single most likely way this
whole ladder fails.**

### 3e-bis. Anti-overfit audit — what was quarried, and what Qud decided

Run explicitly because the author asked for it. Left column: the FEEL a comparable earned its way
into. Right column: the SHAPE, which came from Qud's own register every time.

| Taken as FEEL (from the quarry) | Rejected as SHAPE — and what Qud gave instead |
|---|---|
| Frostpunk: the centre is where survival lives, and growth arrives in legible increments you can pre-position for | **The radial grid, the rings, the forced geometry.** Qud gave: a surveyed *plot*, and a founder whose staked plan beats the grammar anywhere. |
| Against the Storm: the anchor's tier is a function of the settlement, so anchor and sprawl are one system | **The radius, and counting what falls inside it.** Qud gave: the rung reads the city's own demonstrated level — the same thing stage and equilibrium already measure. |
| Anno / Cities: Skylines: a civic centre that visibly becomes grander | **Upgrade-as-replacement, and the town-radius that reorganises the map around it.** Qud gave: *accretion* — the Yd Freehold built over hundreds of years, Ezra as an outgrowth of the Tomb. Nothing is replaced; each rung encloses the last. |
| Farthest Frontier / Factorio: a move priced in labour, done by workers over time | **Instant, universal, free.** Qud gave: a crew that must exist and be capable, world-days that pass whether you are there or not, wear that travels with the stones, and a line in the chronicle. |
| Dwarf Fortress: plan the envelope early, commit the contents late; declare which way a thing grows | Nothing rejected — this one is pure method, and it maps onto footprint-per-tier unchanged. |
| Sim Settlements 2: a settlement that improves itself while you are away | **The pseudo-scrap phase.** Qud gave: the protection law, which is the exact inversion and is not negotiable. |
| Nebuchadnezzar: a monument with a minimum size that the city must make room for | **The monument editor.** Qud gave: the tier declares its footprint; skins carry the expression. |

**The one place I deliberately did not quarry at all:** a prestige/wonder economy. The author's
ruling — one megastructure per city — closes it, and the fiction agrees: Qud's colossi are *singular
places*, not entries in a catalogue of wonders. Ekuemekiyye, Ibul and Yawningmoon are three
arcologies in a whole world, not three you build.

**Honest gap in this pass:** the third parallel research lane (Sim Settlements 2 city-plan depth,
Anno town-radius depth, Songs of Syx monument scaling) did not return before this document was
written. SS2 and Anno are covered from direct searches in §1a; **Songs of Syx monument scaling is
genuinely unresearched here.** Given the anti-overfit steer it is a low-priority gap — Syx's
monuments are a resource-sink-over-time pattern we have already ruled against in a different form
(time-is-labour) — but it is a gap, and it is named rather than papered over.

| Comparable | What it does | What we do instead, and why |
|---|---|---|
| **Frostpunk** | Heart is literal and central; city is forced into rings around it; no relocation; players demolish to reorganise. | We take the *centrality*, refuse the *forced geometry*. | **Manor Lords** | Relocation scoped to buildings whose ideal position depended on information you couldn't have at placement. | Same scoping instinct, different axis: ours is scoped to **ground the heart's ladder was surveyed for**, told at placement. |

The Qud-specific reason all of this holds together: **the founding is a rite, not a placement.**
Water was shared on that ground in front of witnesses. That makes the rite ground the one piece of
geometry in the mod with permanent moral weight — so anchoring the ladder to it, keeping the basin
visible inside the arcology's atrium a thousand days later, and letting the city's centre of
gravity be *pulled back* to it as the great work rises, is the mod's own fiction paying off rather
than a mechanic borrowed from a city-builder.

## 4. Loud rejections — mechanics that look attractive here and would betray the pillars

1. **A research tree gating the heart tiers.** The most natural reading of "supported by research"
   and the most expensive mistake available. `KingdomZoningRules.cs:9-10` states the rule in the
   source: *"This mod has no research tree and does not want one — a tree is a second job, and the
   founder already has one."* Research here means **disks taught and machines certified** — a
   readout, never a spend. Any UI that looks like a tech tree is a pillar violation regardless of
   how good the ladder is.
2. **The heart maturing on elapsed days.** SS2's actual policy is "elapsed days + a happiness
   meter", and Addendum 2 already rejects it by name: *time is labour, never maturation*. A heart
   that grows because a hundred days passed is the witnessed-timer shape ruled out in `fc8c0cb`.
   It must grow because hands carried stone.
3. **A prestige / grandeur / happiness meter driving the tier.** Anno and SS2 both do it; it is the
   most tempting single number in the genre. It is a numbers panel (VISION), and it is
   penalty-for-abstaining by construction — the same reason Terraria's happiness *price penalties*
   were already rejected in the sweep.
4. **The game relocating anything on its own initiative.** Even settlement-raised work. The offer
   shape is not politeness, it is the consent-before-cost pillar; and an auto-move is one bug away
   from being an auto-move of something player-placed. **Offer, disclose the cost, wait.**
5. **A hard reserved ring that refuses construction.** Attractive because it guarantees the ladder
   fits. It contradicts "a staked plan beats the grammar anywhere", it makes the map feel
   pre-authored, and it re-creates Frostpunk's exact complaint. Preference, not prohibition.
6. **Free or instant relocation (the *Move It* shape, unfiltered).** Its 3M subscribers are
   evidence of a real want, not a licence to import its cost model. Free instant moves make
   placement weightless and, in our economy, launder materials out of a strike. Charge labour and
   world-days; carry the wear.
7. **A monument editor / block-by-block design of the great work.** Praised in Nebuchadnezzar, and
   a management screen by definition. The tier declares its footprint; the *skin* system already
   gives expression without giving the founder a CAD tool.
8. **The arcology as a win condition, an end state, or a thing that can be lost.** Both halves are
   wrong. A win screen betrays "loss writes chronicles, not game-overs"; a losable colossus betrays
   the already-ruled megastructure class — **shell is history, function is labour**. An abandoned
   arcology standing dark is the correct failure state and it is Qud's own fiction.
9. **Auto-striking early huts to clear the heart.** The brief's current line — "a heart full of
   early huts is cleared by striking, which is the system working" — is exactly the churn the
   author is now asking to remove. Under this design the heart never needs that ground, and where
   it does, the answer is the offered move, never the automatic strike.
10. **Per-tier upkeep drains on the heart.** Addendum 6 already flags it: a chore timer in robes.
    Maintenance is staff plus the existing upkeep economy, and the megastructure's own rule is that
    *function* withers when unmanned while the *shell* endures.
11. **A move that does not carry identity.** The loudest rejection in this document, because it is
    the failure every comparable commits. Dwarf Fortress: *"There is no such thing as moving a
    workshop"* — the work orders die. Airborne Empire: *"anything you spent on upgrades is lost."*
    Manor Lords: move the Manor and the families go inactive. Against the Storm's move is **free**
    and players still call it *"a pain"* because *"we have to re-add the staff, re setup any
    restrictions and limits, and in general do a ton more clicks"* — and *"you can't move workers.
    They remain where they were."* **A relocation that drops residents, staffing, contents, held
    flags or the building's name is worse than no relocation at all**, because it wears the word
    "move" while doing a strike-and-rebuild. If the identity carry is not complete, do not ship the
    verb.
12. **A fully general move tool.** *Move It*'s three million subscribers are the want; they are not
    the spec. The survey is unambiguous that **scope is what preserves weight** — Manor Lords allows
    it only where the information was unavailable at placement time; Airborne Empire only at a
    skydock; and Terra Nil players say of its late-game general-purpose recycler that it *"makes
    recycling so easy it almost feels like cheating."* Ship the narrow verb behind the offer surface;
    widen later if playtest asks.
13. **Making the heart a slider.** Frostpunk 2 turned heat from a radius you build inside into a
    per-district allocation, and the reaction is the clearest signal in the whole pass: *"you no
    longer build a city — you choose tiles on which the game creates a type of district. **Players
    don't feel like they built the city because they didn't**"*; *"the city builds itself… its
    weird"*; one player *"stopped playing because they removed a core function of the game which is
    balancing the heat mechanics with your city layout."* **When the central structure's effect stops
    being spatial and becomes numeric, the settlement stops being a shape the player authored.**
    Our heart must keep costing ground.
14. **Relocating the heart itself.** Timberborn permits moving the district centre and the cost is
    not the building — it is **re-deriving every relation defined relative to the anchor**, with
    buildings defecting to the wrong district as the standard bug. Our heart feeds siting, roads,
    the gate and reach. And note the absence that is itself a finding: across Against the Storm's
    forums **nobody ever proposes moving the Ancient Hearth** — the immovable heart is so assumed it
    is not even wished against. The rite ground is where water was shared in front of witnesses. It
    does not move.
15. **A wonders menu.** The author's ruling is one megastructure per city, and the failure mode has
    a name in this genre: the late game becomes a list of colossi to tick off, each a prestige sink,
    none of them *the* place. Qud's own arcologies are singular — Ekuemekiyye, Ibul, Yawningmoon are
    three in a world, not three you build. The door the ruling leaves open is for a **different top
    rung on the same single heart** (the city that became its granary; a temple-city), never a second
    colossus raised beside the first. If a design proposal ever needs the words "you could also
    build", it has failed this test.
16. **Town-radius tiers — the specific thing the author warned about.** *"lets not overfit to anno
    too hard."* A civic centre whose tier projects a service radius that reorganises the map around
    it is the single most portable mechanic in this research and it is wrong here twice over: it
    re-imports Against the Storm's #1 complaint (a radius rule colliding with terrain, with nobody
    sure what it gates), and it contradicts the shipped ruling that expansion direction is the
    founder's. Heart-nearness stays a **pull** on siting, exactly as `HeartPull` already is, and
    reach stays the mod's own size×tier ladder (Addendum 6).
17. **Upgrade-as-replacement at the heart.** The genre default — the small hall vanishes, a bigger
    one appears. It is the wrong register for Qud (§3.0), it *creates* the striking churn the author
    asked to remove, and it throws away the best thing the ladder can offer: ground you can walk and
    read the city's whole history off.
18. **Secondary hearts as an unbalanced bypass.** Both comparables offer a "one big heart vs. several
    small ones" fork, and Frostpunk's is broken: Steam Hubs dominate range upgrades so hard that
    players say *"I've never researched any of the range upgrades. Just doesn't seem worth it"* and
    *"Range extension is as useless as usual."* Against the Storm balances its version with fuel
    doubling and a non-producible cost, and the debate stays live. **If this mod ever adds a second
    heart** (a district shrine, a satellite plaza), it must not make the ladder skippable — or the
    grandest rung becomes the thing nobody builds, which is failure mode 3e(iv) arriving by a
    different door.

## 5. Open questions for the author

1. **Does the heart gate the catalogue?** A very common genre pattern (Anno's town hall, the
   mobile-builder "upgrade the hall to unlock the tier") is *the central building gates what else
   you may build*. It would give the ladder enormous pull. It is **not recommended**: we already
   gate on `GrowthStage` + derived `TechLevel` + district + stratum, and routing all of it through
   one building makes a single point of failure out of the one structure the player is most
   attached to. Flagged rather than rejected because it is the author's call whether the heart
   should *add* a gate (e.g. XL civic designs need the great court standing) as opposed to
   *replacing* the existing ones.
2. **How big is the surveyed heart ground, and is it visible from turn one?** Showing the full
   arcology footprint at Camp is either thrilling ("this is what this could become") or
   presumptuous ("the game has already decided"). Two settings; needs an author feel-call. A middle
   option: survey only **one rung ahead**, and reveal the next as the previous is earned.
3. **Should a second settlement's rite ground get the same ladder?** The roster wave makes cities
   plural. Either every city can climb (and most will not), or the ladder is the capital's
   privilege. This is a fiction question — is the arcology *the* place, or *a* place? Note it is
   *orthogonal* to the one-per-city ruling, which caps colossi within a city, not across the realm.
4. **Does the move verb generalise beyond the heart?** Everything in §2a works for any plot. The
   narrow version (only yielding plots, only on a ring call) is safer; the general version ("move
   this house over two cells") is the thing *Move It*'s three million subscribers actually want.
   Recommendation: ship the narrow one, keep the general one behind the same offer-and-consent
   surface so it can be opened later without new machinery.
5. **What is the arcology called?** "Arcology" is canon (Ekuemekiyye, Ibul, Yawningmoon) but those
   are the True Kin's. The founder's own would want a name in Qud's morphology, and the naming
   moment is a chronicle accomplishment worth designing.
6. **What would a specialised end-state actually have to prove?** The ruling leaves the door open
   for a city that became its granary, or a temple-city, with the burden of proof on the design.
   Suggested bar, for the author to accept or raise: it must be **a different rung 5 on the same
   single heart** (never a second structure), it must be **reachable only by how the city was
   actually played** (the granary-city is a city that spent a century feeding others, read off the
   ledgers it already keeps), and it must **cost the same ground** — so it is a different ending,
   not an extra one.
